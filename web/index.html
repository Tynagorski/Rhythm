The argument

Effort was never your problem.

You will always feed the business. That part takes care of itself — it has deadlines, other people, and a calendar to enforce it. What quietly starves is everything with no meeting attached to it.

So Rhythm does not measure how much you did. It measures how much of your life the day reached. Toggle the chips above and try to score well on a day that was all business. You can get to 82 — while four fifths of your life got nothing. That gap is the whole product.

Calendar integrity

An app that makes you update your calendar.

Every planner is downstream of one thing: whether your calendar is telling the truth. Rhythm counts the days since you last looked at the week ahead. Past your interval — three days by default — it starts nudging, and the nudge escalates for three days before it stops. An alert that never stops is an alert you turn off.

Overloaded 6h 30m of meetings against an 8h day on Wednesday.
No room Longest clear stretch on Tuesday is 35m. Deep work needs a runway.

The audit reads your calendar and nothing else. It looks for the things that quietly rot a week: double bookings, invitations you never answered, days booked past the hard stop you set, and weekdays with nothing on them at all — which almost always means an out-of-date calendar rather than a free day.

Rhythm never writes to your calendar. Access is read-only and there is no write path anywhere in the app. It tells you what is wrong; you fix it where you already work.

Privacy

There is nowhere to send your data, by design.

No account. No sign-in. No analytics SDK. No cloud sync. Your plans, your rituals and everything derived from your calendar live in a container on your device.

Every notification is scheduled locally, so the whole daily loop works in airplane mode. Calendar events are read into memory, analysed, and discarded — only the derived numbers are ever written down. Read the policy.

Questions

The things people actually ask.

Is there an app that reminds you to update your calendar?

That is the feature Rhythm was built around. It tracks how long it has been since you last reviewed the week ahead, and once you pass your chosen interval it starts nudging — escalating for three days, then stopping. The review itself walks you through conflicts, unanswered invites, days booked past your hard stop, and weekdays with nothing on them.

Does Rhythm change my calendar events?

No. Calendar access is read-only and there is no write path anywhere in the app. Rhythm reads existing events, analyses them on device, and tells you what needs fixing. You make the change in your own calendar app.

What is a work-life balance score, exactly?

A 0–100 daily number built from four measured parts: weighted completion of what you committed to (30%), rituals kept versus due (25%), how many of five life domains the day touched (25%), and whether your boundaries held (20%). The breakdown is always shown next to the number, because a single opaque score is something to game rather than something to act on.

How is this different from a to-do list?

A to-do list measures how much you did. Rhythm measures how much of your life the day reached. It caps you at one keystone and three momentum tasks, and weights the five domains so a day spent entirely on business cannot score well however much got finished.

Does it need an account or an internet connection?

Neither. There is no sign-in and no server. Every notification is scheduled on device, so the daily loop works offline.

What happens if I skip a habit on purpose?

A deliberate skip shrinks the denominator instead of counting as a miss, and pauses your streak rather than ending it. The app is trying to get an honest record out of you, not a padded one.

Which iPhones does it run on?

iOS 17 and later, which covers iPhone XS and newer. The interactive widgets and the balance chart both need iOS 17 APIs.

When can I actually use it?
